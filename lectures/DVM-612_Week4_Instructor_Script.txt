DVM 612 Week 4 instructor script (updated, no ECFVG)


SLIDE 1. DVM 612 · PRINCIPLES OF SURGERY · WEEK 4
    SPEAK: Welcome. This is Week 4 of DVM 612. Weeks 1–3 covered asepsis, sterilization, facilities, monitoring, communication, and surgery reports. Today we put the patient in the center: decide if they are a surgical candidate, prepare them and yourselves aseptically, and own the 24 hours after the last suture. One hour. Three acts. Keep Fossum nearby. Lab and OSCE will ask you to demonstrate the same prep and discharge skills.

SLIDE 2. By the end of this hour you will be able to
    SPEAK: Read the five objectives aloud. Tell students Exam 1 (Week 6) will sample this material. OSCE/lab sign-off will include patient prep and discharge writing (LO 4 and 7).

SLIDE 3. Sixty-minute plan
    SPEAK: Keep a visible timer. If discussion runs, protect Part II (prep) — that is what lab and OSCE actually watch.

SLIDE 4. Why this hour is not optional
    SPEAK: DVM 612 will hold you to this: a beautiful closure that bleeds in recovery is a failed surgery. The first 24 hours are part of the operation.

SLIDE 5. Halsted’s principles — the through-line for today
    SPEAK: Do not spend more than 2 minutes. Point: every Halsted principle has a preop or postop action, not just an intraoperative one.

SLIDE 6. The perioperative continuum
    SPEAK: The red thread: you do not hand off responsibility at the OR door. Anesthesia gets the dog ready for surgical prep; surgery owns prep through the last skin suture; both own recovery.

SLIDE 7. Preoperative evaluation and the peri-operative plan
    SPEAK: Transition. Ask: who has watched an elective surgery get cancelled at induction? That is a successful preop exam.

SLIDE 8. Goals of the preoperative evaluation
    SPEAK: Four jobs. Students often skip #3 and #4. Elective surgery on an unstable patient is not bravery.

SLIDE 9. History that actually changes the plan
    SPEAK: Spend 2 minutes. Highlight Doberman/vWD, brachycephalics, and last meal as exam favorites. Never premedicate before you have examined the patient.

SLIDE 10. Physical examination — do it yourself
    SPEAK: Never skip PE because the technician already did vitals. DVM 612 students share patients with anesthesia. The surgeon still owns the decision to proceed.

SLIDE 11. ASA physical status — memorize this wording
    SPEAK: Read ASA I and III slowly. The emergency E modifier is used clinically (ASA IV-E for GDV). An elective procedure on an ASA IV patient is a decision, not a schedule slot. Next slides: a real ASA III Cocker Spaniel.

SLIDE 12. Assign the ASA — 60-second cases
    SPEAK: Cold-call four students. C is the real ASA III we will unpack. D is the trap: do not induce a hyperkalemic blocked cat because surgery is on the board.

SLIDE 13. Real ASA III case — Cocker Spaniel with vestibular disease
    SPEAK: This is a real peri-operative patient, not a cartoon. Spend 90 seconds. Cockers plus dirty ears plus new circling is otitis media/interna until proven otherwise. Intact TM is good news for topical safety only if you are sure the middle ear is not involved — you are not sure. Knuckling vs drunk vestibular ataxia: have a student demonstrate paw placing. If placing is truly delayed, do not call this ‘just an ear’ and induce for a TECA tonight.

SLIDE 14. Why this dog is ASA III — and what changes today
    SPEAK: This is the ASA III teaching case for Exam 1. Punchline: vestibular + severe otitis is moderate/severe compensated disease. You may still anesthetize — for CT, flush, or TECA — but you do not treat the dog like a healthy spay. No Animax-style aminoglycoside in a suspect inner ear. No NSAID after DexSP.

SLIDE 15. Preoperative diagnostics — indicated, not automatic
    SPEAK: Avoid dogma. Requesting nothing in a geriatric patient is as wrong as a $800 panel on every puppy. Know your hospital policy and be able to defend it.

SLIDE 16. Stabilize before you sterilize
    SPEAK: One sentence: resuscitation is not delayed for clipping. Elective OHE is delayed for pyoderma. GDV is not delayed for a dental cleaning that was also booked.

SLIDE 17. Fasting — traditional teaching vs. current practice
    SPEAK: I teach AAHA-style 4–6 h for healthy small animals as best practice, and I tell them older exam banks may still say overnight NPO. Ruminant contrast prevents a species-blind answer on Exam 1.

SLIDE 18. Informed consent is a surgical skill
    SPEAK: Students under-consent electives. A 2-minute risk talk prevents a 2-hour complaint. Mention DNR explicitly.

SLIDE 19. Checklist and the analgesia plan belong in preop
    SPEAK: Checklist takes 90 seconds and prevents wrong-site and forgotten cefazolin. Analgesia: locals are underused by students.

SLIDE 20. Surgical antimicrobial prophylaxis — stewardship, not ritual
    SPEAK: High-yield Exam 1 slide. Elective canine OHE is a clean procedure — do not invent a 14-day cephalexin prescription. If you give cefazolin, it is IV around induction, not a souvenir bottle home. Contrast with the vestibular Cocker: that ear is infected, so antimicrobials are therapy.

SLIDE 21. Knowledge check — preoperative
    SPEAK: Take 2 minutes. Then move. Do not let this become a 10-minute debate.

SLIDE 22. Patient and surgeon preparation
    SPEAK: This is the skill cluster lab will watch: hair removal, skin prep, positioning, scrubbing/attire, gowning/gloving, draping. Lost asepsis that nobody names is how patients get SSI.

SLIDE 23. What we actually score in patient and surgeon prep
    SPEAK: Read the six scored boxes. Students should be able to name them on Exam 1 without the OHE steps mixed in.

SLIDE 24. Sequence — do not invert the rooms
    SPEAK: Classic fail: clipping in the OR, or sterile prep in the prep room then dragging a wet dog across a dirty corridor without a clean transfer. Another fail: starting the clip before a surgical plane — patient wakes, contaminates, gets clipper lacerations.

SLIDE 25. Hair removal
    SPEAK: Demonstrate with hands on your own abdomen if no model. Missed hair at the drape edge fails the prep.

SLIDE 26. Know the field before you pick up the clippers
    SPEAK: One minute. Orthopedic hang-prep is the concept they need: the foot is dirty, wrapped, and you prep from incision toward the foot, never reverse.

SLIDE 27. Skin antiseptics — choose with anatomy, not loyalty
    SPEAK: Faculty preference at LIU labs may be CHG–alcohol for trunk skin. Hammer ocular/ear contraindications — they have already seen ototoxic ear meds in other contexts.

SLIDE 28. How to scrub the patient
    SPEAK: If time, mime the spiral on the lectern. Mention hanging-limb prep: start at incision, work distally toward the dirty foot, then the wrapped foot is handed off.

SLIDE 29. Positioning is physiology, not just ‘on its back’
    SPEAK: Canine OHE is dorsal recumbency. Over-splitting femurs of a large dog is a student habit. Mention GDV: even positioning can worsen caval compression — communicate with anesthesia.

SLIDE 30. Four-quadrant draping
    SPEAK: Four-quadrant is not optional style. Demonstrate air-cuffing the drape. Hair at the edge means re-clip or re-drape.

SLIDE 31. Surgeon preparation
    SPEAK: Practice gown packs in lab until it is muscle memory. Talking through a contaminated glove replacement is an Exam 1 question.

SLIDE 32. Breaks in asepsis — recognize, announce, fix
    SPEAK: Culture in lab: we praise people who say I just contaminated my sleeve. We do not praise silent heroics.

SLIDE 33. Never-events — these stop the surgery
    SPEAK: Connect #1, #2, #3 to today’s lecture. Intra-op details (#4) are later weeks, but ureter injury is a positioning/exposure/identification problem that starts with a calm, well-prepped patient.

SLIDE 34. Knowledge check — preparation
    SPEAK: C is the safety slide. D is the nuanced yes.

SLIDE 35. Postoperative care
    SPEAK: Shift energy. Students think postop is ‘the techs’ job.’ It is not.

SLIDE 36. Immediate recovery — the surgery is not over
    SPEAK: ABC plus temperature plus pain. Hemorrhage speech: do not send a pale recovering OHE to the kennel because ‘she’s just groggy.’

SLIDE 37. Pain assessment is a vital sign
    SPEAK: Course CC3. Mention they will practice scoring in skills labs. NSAIDs: not in hypovolemia, kidney injury, GI ulcer, concurrent steroids.

SLIDE 38. The incision after you leave it
    SPEAK: Licking and basketball-with-the-dog are the two discharge failures. Be concrete: ‘no off-leash for 14 days.’

SLIDE 39. The first 24 hours — what fails a surgery after the OR
    SPEAK: Spend time on hemorrhage vs. seroma (students confuse them) and skin vs. fascial dehiscence. Evisceration protocol in one breath.

SLIDE 40. Surgical report — if it is not written, it was not done
    SPEAK: LO 7. Lab assignment later: write a report and discharge for the model/cadaver/live lab. Show them you grade completeness, not handwriting art.

SLIDE 41. Discharge instructions — LO 7 in the client’s language
    SPEAK: Role-play one sentence: ‘If the incision opens and you see fat or intestine, cover with a clean wet towel and come now.’ Students write too much physiology and too few phone numbers.

SLIDE 42. When to come back tonight — teach this verbatim
    SPEAK: Pale gums + distended abdomen after OHE is never ‘wait until morning.’

SLIDE 43. Fluids, feeding, and the rest of the animal
    SPEAK: Cats and food. Diabetics and insulin. Short.

SLIDE 44. Large animal footnote (Week 13 will go deep)
    SPEAK: Do not lecture LA for 10 minutes. Plant the flag that Halsted travels to the barn.

SLIDE 45. Integrated case — 8 minutes
    SPEAK: Walk it without taking questions until the end. This is the mental movie for lab.

SLIDE 46. Exam 1 pearls
    SPEAK: These six are fair game for MCQ and short answer. Stop here if time is gone.

SLIDE 47. Take-home
    SPEAK: Close the loop to slide 1.

SLIDE 48. References & source map
    SPEAK: Point to Fossum as the night-before reading.

SLIDE 49. Questions
    SPEAK: Take questions. If none: ‘What will you do differently on your next observed prep?’ Dismiss on time.
