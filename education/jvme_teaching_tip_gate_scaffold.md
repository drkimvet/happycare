## Abstract

Licensed veterinary formularies and retrieval-based clinical decision-support tools now supply many milligram-per-kilogram numbers that students used to memorize. The educational problem is no longer “the student cannot find a dose.” It is that a retrieved dose can still be the wrong act: wrong species, wrong localization, outdated source, or a drug that is on-label for a different patient. This Teaching Tip describes a six-gate scaffold the author uses in small animal emergency teaching. The gates force a visible Type 2 check before any number is written: species, localization, refuse a silent corpus, outdated versus current source, the datum that would flip the plan, and consent when the disease is decompressible. Students must produce a six-line answer. They may look up a monograph only after the gates. The scaffold is grounded in dual-process clinical reasoning and cognitive-load design. It is a teaching method, not an outcome study. Formal learner evaluation is planned and is not reported here.

**Keywords:** clinical reasoning; dual-process theory; cognitive load; emergency medicine; formulary; artificial intelligence; competency-based veterinary education

## The problem that changed

Veterinary educators have long treated clinical reasoning as the hard problem and the formulary as the easy one. May argued that the fundamental challenge is to help students reason safely on both familiar and unfamiliar cases, combining pattern recognition (Type 1) with analytic checks (Type 2).[^1] That problem has not gone away. The environment around it has changed.

Students now meet licensed monographs, interaction checkers, and retrieval-based decision-support products that return a cited milligram-per-kilogram. Ambient scribes capture the SOAP. None of those tools is a license, and none of them localizes a soft abdomen plus a urine output of about 1 mL/kg/hr plus unilateral renomegaly to the kidney or ureter that actually needs a plan. A retrieved dose can be chemically correct and clinically wrong.

Two student errors dominate the author’s emergency teaching:

1. **Number first.** The student opens a formulary before naming the species-specific disease and the anatomic space.
2. **Corpus as permission.** The student treats “the drug is in the monograph” as “this patient should receive that drug tonight.”

A third error is quieter and older: treating a 2013 emergency table as 2026 standard of care.

These are not information-retrieval failures. They are reasoning failures. Teaching more monographs will not fix them. Teaching students to *refuse an invented or misapplied number* might.

This paper does not compare products and does not claim that any commercial tool should be opened in class. It describes a scaffold for the judgment those tools cannot do.

## Educational theory

### Dual-process reasoning, not “intuition bad, analysis good”

Eva summarized the evidence that expert diagnosis uses both non-analytic (Type 1) and analytic (Type 2) strategies, and that teachers should train flexible use of both rather than suppress pattern recognition.[^2] Croskerry’s universal model places the same two systems in series: pattern recognition first, then a discretionary override when the case is atypical, high-stakes, or poorly calibrated.[^3] May translated this for veterinary curricula: combined approaches outperform one system alone, and Type 1 reasoning is not a vice if students are taught when to triangulate.[^1] Norman and colleagues later argued that many “bias” stories are knowledge or calibration problems, not proof that Type 1 thinking should be banned.[^4]

The scaffold treats Type 1 as useful and dangerous in the same breath. A male cat that is straining *should* trigger “urethral obstruction until the bladder is empty.” A cat with garlic ingestion *should not* trigger “lily-style acute kidney injury within a few hours.” The gates are the override points: short, named checks that make Type 2 visible in a noisy emergency service.

### Cognitive load: reduce the search, keep the decision

Van Merriënboer and Sweller advised health-profession teachers to limit extraneous load and to scaffold intrinsic load so working memory can be spent on the schema that matters.[^5] Asking a novice to generate a complete problem list, a ranked differential, a fluid recipe, and a dose from memory in one breath overloads the channel. Asking the same novice for six constrained lines (gate, localization, do-not, do-next, dose rule, flip datum) is a worked-example structure. The formulary search is postponed until the schema is on the page. That is a load decision, not a technology ban.

### Competency, not coverage

The AAVMC Competency-Based Veterinary Education framework asks programs to develop clinical reasoning and professional judgment, not topic coverage alone.[^6] Entrustable acts in emergency practice include deciding *not* to give a drug that a classmate just retrieved. The scaffold makes that refusal a graded performance, not an aside.

Humm and May found that first-opinion clinicians and students often undervalue Type 1 reasoning and that peri-consultation talk rarely develops reasoning even when both parties say they want it.[^7] Vinten and colleagues reported that graduates can still feel a reasoning deficit when they enter practice, in part because they held little real decision responsibility as students.[^8] A required six-line answer is one way to make the talk happen in under three minutes and to give the student a decision they must own.

## The six gates

Each gate is a yes/no or one-sentence Type 2 check. Students may not write a milligram-per-kilogram until the gates are on the page. If a public guideline or the hospital protocol does not name a number, the required token is “confirm in the licensed formulary or hospital protocol” (the author marks this △). Inventing a number to look decisive is a failing move.

### Gate 1. Species first

No species, no dose. True *Lilium* or *Hemerocallis* in a cat is an acute kidney-injury emergency; the same plant in a dog is not that protocol. Garlic and onion are delayed Heinz-body hemolysis, not a few-hour nephrotoxin. Oral beta-lactams in hamsters, guinea pigs, and rabbits are a hard stop. The Type 1 flash (“sick cat, start fluids and a steroid”) is exactly what this gate interrupts.

### Gate 2. Localize the space before you treat the abdomen or the chest

Unilateral renomegaly, a soft belly, and a urine output around 1 mL/kg/hr localize to that kidney or ureter until proven otherwise. Free abdominal fluid is not “drain it” until paired fluid and serum creatinine and potassium are known. Tense cranial abdomen for days is pancreas, stomach, biliary tree, or cranial small intestine, not tonight’s snack, until the timeline is written down. Two animals that shared a meal are two localizations. Open-mouth breathing in a cat is a space problem (upper airway, bronchial, pleural, cardiogenic edema, anemia, arterial thromboembolism, anaphylaxis) before it is a syringe problem.

### Gate 3. Refuse a silent corpus

If the number is not on a named public guideline or the stated hospital protocol, the student writes △ and stops. Three published N-acetylcysteine families (acetaminophen, a xylitol conversation, established hepatic failure) are not one drip. A cubic-centimeter draw is a volume, not a dose, until this vial’s milligram-per-milliliter and the body weight are on the note.

### Gate 4. Outdated versus current

A 2013 emergency chapter is a study book, not automatically 2026 care. Students must name the newer public source when one exists: IRIS acute kidney injury grading,[^9] AAHA fluid therapy 2024,[^10] ISCAID urinary guidelines 2019,[^11] the ACVIM feline pancreatitis consensus,[^12] and the 2024 RECOVER CPR update.[^13] They must also flag chemistry errors that survive in older print (zinc phosphide liberates phosphine, not phosgene). The learning target is source dating, not contempt for books.

### Gate 5. Name the datum that flips the plan

Every answer names the next observation that would change the recommendation: quantified urine output rather than a wet pad, contralateral kidney function, fluid-to-serum creatinine, glucose, a typed blood product, or a TFAST space. This is Croskerry’s override made concrete.[^3] It also trains students to stop treating the first laboratory number as a verdict (one lactate value is not a euthanasia line; one weak feline pancreatic lipase snap is not a diagnosis[^12]).

### Gate 6. Consent when the disease is decompressible

Hydronephrosis, pyonephrosis, urethral obstruction, gastric dilatation-volvulus, pyometra, tension pneumothorax, and septic abdomen are referral or surgery conversations. Antibiotics, a trocar, or “watch the belly” are not substitutes for naming the procedure the owner is accepting or declining. Professional identity includes the pediatric-advocate role May described: the animal cannot consent, so the student must put the decompressible problem in a sentence the client can refuse.[^1]

## Required answer shape

Students answer in six lines. Slogan titles and decorative “take-home” banners are not accepted. The shape is the assessment.

```
1) Gate: species / hard stop / △
2) Localization: one sentence
3) Do not: 2–4 bullets
4) Do next: 2–4 bullets, including the flip datum
5) Dose: licensed formulary or hospital protocol, or omit
6) Source: named public guideline, or △
```

The “Do not” line is not negativity for its own sake. It is the Type 2 override written where a classmate can see it. Common required don’ts in the author’s service include: no dexamethasone sodium phosphate or NSAID on an azotemic cat; do not default a cefazolin continuous-rate infusion because the patient is sick; do not bolus a potassium-containing bag;[^10] name the shock type before a fluid bolus (cardiogenic failure is not empty-vessel shock); do not treat tamponade as a furosemide protocol; anaphylaxis is epinephrine first, not diphenhydramine.

## How to run it

### Fifty-minute classroom or rounds block

Table 3 is a single session that fits a core small animal medicine or surgery hour, a clinical-skills lab, or evening emergency rounds.

1. **Five minutes.** One sentence of theory: Type 1 will fire; the gates are the override; a retrieved dose is not a plan.
2. **Five minutes.** Show the six-line blank. Ban milligram-per-kilogram in the first draft.
3. **Twenty-five minutes.** Two vignettes, students write in pairs, then one pair reads only the “Do not” and “flip datum” lines. The teacher does not supply a secret dose.
4. **Ten minutes.** Open the licensed formulary *after* the gates. Compare “the drug exists” with “this patient tonight.”
5. **Five minutes.** Name one outdated-versus-current pair from that week’s reading (for example, a 2013 CPR habit versus RECOVER 2024 withdrawal of high-dose epinephrine).[^13]

### What the teacher says, verbatim

- “Species before syringe.”
- “Name the space.”
- “If you cannot name the source, you do not write the number.”
- “What one result would make you abandon this plan?”
- “Is this decompressible? Then the owner conversation is part of the answer.”

### What the teacher does not do

- Do not grade speed of formulary lookup as clinical reasoning.
- Do not harvest copyrighted textbook tables into the slide deck. Cite the guideline. Send students to the licensed monograph for the number.
- Do not put owner names, chart numbers, or identifiable case photographs in shared teaching files.
- Do not require students to open any commercial decision-support product. The point of the hour is the gate, not the brand.
- Do not treat bilingual compact answers as lesser work. A correct six-line answer in the student’s stronger language beats a fluent wrong plan.
- Do not move live emergency cases onto a didactic slide set that was already locked for another course.

### Assessment that matches the method

A passing script has all six lines, a localization that a colleague could act on, at least one specific don’t, a flip datum, and either a sourced number or a visible △. A failing script is a differential list followed by an unsourced dose. Peer review of the “Do not” line, as used in other case-based reasoning electives,[^14] works in a large room because it is short.

This paper reports no scores. Programs that wish to evaluate the scaffold should obtain ethics approval first and pre-specify the outcome (script concordance, think-aloud coding, or safety-event language). JVME does not treat an unevaluated pilot as educational research, and this manuscript does not pretend to be one.

## Limitations and next work

The scaffold is one clinician-educator’s method, developed in small animal emergency practice and used when teaching veterinary students. It has not been tested against another reasoning curriculum. It is species-heavy toward dog and cat because that is the author’s night work; equine and production-animal courses would need their own hard stops. It does not replace primary surgical or medical content. It also does not compete with a licensed formulary. Claiming that a teacher or a language model can “out-Plumb” a monograph is how students get hurt.

The honest next paper is an Educational Research Report: ethics approval, a pre-specified rubric, and learner work samples with identifiers removed. Until that exists, colleagues should treat this as a Teaching Tip: steal the six lines, keep the theory, and refuse the invented number.

---

## Conclusion

Retrieval tools moved the easy part of emergency teaching (finding a number) off the student’s desk. The hard part is unchanged and more visible: species, space, source, and whether this patient should receive the drug at all. A six-gate answer makes that judgment teachable in the time a service actually has. Neither a monograph nor a decision-support product replaces a veterinary license. The student still has to decide.

## Table 1. Six gates as a dual-process scaffold

| Gate | Type 1 trigger (useful flash) | Type 2 check (must be spoken) | Teacher prompt |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Species | “Sick cat, start treatment.” | Wrong species protocol? Exotic oral beta-lactam? | Species before syringe. |
| 2. Space | “Acute abdomen. Tap.” / “Dyspnea. Lasix.” | Which organ or cavity? Same house ≠ same localization. | Name the space. |
| 3. Silent corpus | “I can probably remember the dose.” | Named guideline or hospital protocol? Volume ≠ dose. | No source, no number. |
| 4. Date | “The chapter I read said…” | What is the dated public source? | 2013 is a book, not a year of care. |
| 5. Flip | “The first number decides.” | What result kills this plan? | Name the flip datum. |
| 6. Consent | “Medical management overnight.” | Is the lesion decompressible? | Say the procedure the owner can refuse. |

## Table 2. Composite teaching vignettes (not medical records)

**Vignette A. Azotemic cat, right renomegaly, urine output about 1 mL/kg/hr, soft abdomen.**  
Failing script: dexamethasone, NSAID, drain the belly, cefazolin continuous-rate infusion, aluminum hydroxide while anorexic.  
Passing gates: feline acute kidney injury frame; localize to the right kidney or ureter; do not drain without paired creatinine and potassium; quantified urine output is not oliguria at ~1 mL/kg/hr; pain control is part of care (named opioid, then formulary); decompressible disease needs a referral sentence.

**Vignette B. Two cats, one household, shared seasoned jerky; one has days of cranial abdominal pain and a weak-positive feline pancreatic lipase snap; the other is tired only.**  
Failing script: copy one discharge, write “below the toxic threshold,” withhold food for 12 hours, give gabapentin instead of an opioid to the painful cat, treat garlic as lily-type kidney injury tonight.  
Passing gates: two patients, two localizations; snap is supportive, not a diagnosis;[^12] allium is a delayed hemolysis clock; do not NPO a vomiting cat; opioid is the acute analgesic conversation; cubic centimeters are a volume until the vial is named.

**Vignette C. Open-mouth cat, expiratory push, quiet chest.**  
Failing script: furosemide plus albuterol plus dexamethasone as a cocktail, wrestle for a ventrodorsal film, send home as anxiety.  
Passing gates: oxygen and hands off; name the space; quiet restrictive chest is pleural until tapped; dexamethasone still forbidden if the cat is azotemic; do not harvest puff-count tables from memory.
