Do not copy a drug number from this packet as a standing order. Confirm every milligram-per-kilogram in Plumb or the hospital protocol.

Owner identity, phone numbers, emails, street addresses, clinician names, and clinic names have been removed. Dates are given as they appear on the laboratory and visit records. The records are incomplete and sometimes inconsistent, like the charts you will inherit. Noticing what is missing is part of the exercise.

How a doctor reads a chart

Keep a running problem list. After each stage write: what is new, how the differential changed, and the next test. Sort every sentence you write into one of four boxes:

Box Meaning Example that belongs here
Observed finding A number, sign, or sentence that is actually in the record Creatinine 0.3 mg/dL (Jan 2022)
Interpretation What that finding might mean Low creatinine can reflect small muscle mass or polyuria
Causal hypothesis A proposed why that still needs a test Cortisol excess is making the urine dilute
Diagnostic conclusion A named disease with the test that proved it A named UTI with a cystocentesis culture

A laboratory abnormality is not automatically the cause of the presenting complaint. A treatment response supports a hypothesis. It does not prove it. Two things that change in the same week cannot be assigned one cause.

Therapeutic hierarchy for this course: identify and treat confirmed medical abnormalities while you relieve itch, pain, and sleep disruption. Reassess. Do not assume that a medical label or a behavioral label accounts for the whole dog.

Why these two cases

Owners rarely say “my dog has a thyroid problem” or “my dog’s calcium is off.” They say the dog freezes in the lobby, or paces all night and seems lost. Those are behavioral complaints. They are easy to label as training, anxiety, or old age.

The lecture idea is that behavior is an integrative endpoint of multiple physiologic systems. When a behavior is uncontrollable or refractory to environmental change and training, ask what is driving it from underneath before you treat the behavior itself.

Oliver is the case in which a building visit looks like “drama,” and the physiology is a three-step sympathetic storm. The same dog was hospitalized for parvovirus at 11 weeks. Coco is the case in which the owner sees night-time restlessness and “dementia,” and the chart underneath is a pile of endocrine, urinary, cardiopulmonary, and mineral problems. The first urinalysis looks like a urinary tract infection. The renal values stay normal. That combination is the trap.

Background to have in mind:

  • The HPA axis mediates the endocrine stress response. A normal stress response is not the same as pathologic cortisol excess. Cortisol excess needs a clinical picture and a validated test.
  • Thyroid hormone has broad metabolic and dermatologic effects. Links between canine hypothyroidism and a specific behavioral phenotype are less well established than the skin and coat signs.
  • Calcium is tightly regulated. When calcium falls, PTH rises. Vitamin D status and dietary calcium-to-phosphorus ratio determine how well that system can compensate. Calcium also sets neuronal excitability.
  • Itch is not only a skin event. IL-31 acts on sensory nerves. Whether that applies in a given dog has to be shown, not assumed.

Case 1. Oliver

Situational phobia, a three-step sympathetic storm, and a parvo hospitalization at 11 weeks

This section is built from the short Integration Medicine III Oliver deck (7 slides). The overview lecture also names a 2-year-old mixed-breed dog with panic confined to elevator use and a normal CBC, chemistry, and thyroid profile. Stage O1. The building sequence (printed as “Investigating Oliver’s situational phobia”)

The short deck walks the body through three places in one outing. Treat each line as an observed finding first, then as a proposed circuit.

Place Observed finding on the slide Circuit named on the slide Interpretation named on the slide
Hallway Panting, mydriasis, tachycardia, scanning the environment Locus coeruleus–adrenal axis Early sympathetic activation; heightened arousal; acute anxiety or catecholamine surge
Lobby Refused to walk further, stood still; the owner carried him Periaqueductal gray (PAG) and amygdalo-cortical circuits Behavioral freezing; motor inhibition from fear paralysis
Concierge Hyperthermia, severe tachycardia, sweaty paw pads Sympathoadrenal hyperactivation Catecholamine surge with a thermogenic and cardiovascular response

This is still a stress response until a validated adrenal test says otherwise. Hyperthermia and sweaty pads at the concierge are not a fever workup by themselves, and they are not proof of heatstroke. They are the same axis turning up.

Teaching point: The hallway is early sympathetic activation. The lobby is freezing. The concierge is a catecholamine surge you can see on the pads. A parvo hospitalization at 11 weeks is history that belongs on the problem list. It is not, by itself, the diagnosis of the freeze. Drug class follows the circuit you have actually localized.

Management principles (not a recipe):

  • Localize the step (hallway versus lobby versus concierge) before you name a drug class.
  • Desensitization and counterconditioning start at the earliest step the dog can still eat.
  • Situational anxiolysis or a serotonergic trial is a hospital-protocol conversation.
  • Reassess after the context is trained. If the dog now panics everywhere, reopen the medical screen and the differential on the problem-list slide.

Pause and think

  1. How does a dog with a bacterial UTI have a normal creatinine and BUN? What does a normal creatinine fail to exclude?
  2. Why is cystocentesis the preferred sample, and what would a free-catch sample change?
  3. Dilute urine and glucocorticoid excess both predispose to UTI. Explain why. Does that change how long you treat?
  4. Distinguish bacterial cystitis, pyelonephritis, subclinical bacteriuria, and sterile cystitis. Which would you treat, and on what evidence?
  5. Lethargy, inappetence, and low-normal blood pressure appeared within weeks of several new drugs. List every plausible cause and say how you would separate them.
  6. A UTI is treated and her renal values are normal. Is the polyuria explained? Why or why not?
  7. The chart names two different organisms one day apart. What do you write in the problem list tonight: “UTI,” “possible UTI,” or “conflicting culture reports”?

What a doctor does not do with this stage: treat the UTI, see the creatinine stay “normal,” and close the polyuria. Lower urinary infection can sit on top of dilute urine. It does not create eight cups of water a day by itself unless you have also proved pyelonephritis or another concentrating defect.
