## How a doctor reads a chart

Keep a running problem list. After each stage write: what is new, how the differential changed, and the next test. Sort every sentence you write into one of four boxes:

| Box | Meaning | Example that belongs here |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Observed finding | A number, sign, or sentence that is actually in the record | Creatinine 0.3 mg/dL (Jan 2022) |
| Interpretation | What that finding might mean | Low creatinine can reflect small muscle mass or polyuria |
| Causal hypothesis | A proposed why that still needs a test | Cortisol excess is making the urine dilute |
| Diagnostic conclusion | A named disease with the test that proved it | A named UTI with a cystocentesis culture |

A laboratory abnormality is not automatically the cause of the presenting complaint. A treatment response supports a hypothesis. It does not prove it. Two things that change in the same week cannot be assigned one cause.

Therapeutic hierarchy for this course: identify and treat confirmed medical abnormalities while you relieve itch, pain, and sleep disruption. Reassess. Do not assume that a medical label or a behavioral label accounts for the whole dog.

## Why these two cases

Owners rarely say "my dog has a thyroid problem" or "my dog's calcium is off." They say the dog panics in the elevator, or paces all night and seems lost. Those are behavioral complaints. They are easy to label as training, anxiety, or old age.

The lecture idea is that **behavior is an integrative endpoint of multiple physiologic systems**. When a behavior is uncontrollable or refractory to environmental change and training, ask what is driving it from underneath before you treat the behavior itself.

Oliver is the case in which the medical screen is normal and the physiology is still the point: a conditioned autonomic storm in one context. Coco is the case in which the owner sees night-time restlessness and "dementia," and the chart underneath is a pile of endocrine, urinary, cardiopulmonary, and mineral problems. The first urinalysis looks like a urinary tract infection. The renal values stay normal. That combination is the trap.

Background to have in mind:

- The HPA axis mediates the endocrine stress response. A normal stress response is not the same as pathologic cortisol excess. Cortisol excess needs a clinical picture and a validated test.
- Thyroid hormone has broad metabolic and dermatologic effects. Links between canine hypothyroidism and a specific behavioral phenotype are less well established than the skin and coat signs.
- Calcium is tightly regulated. When calcium falls, PTH rises. Vitamin D status and dietary calcium-to-phosphorus ratio determine how well that system can compensate. Calcium also sets neuronal excitability.
- Itch is not only a skin event. IL-31 acts on sensory nerves. Whether that applies in a given dog has to be shown, not assumed.

## Case 1. Oliver

### Situational phobia with physiologic amplification

**Signalment (lecture case):** 2-year-old mixed-breed dog.

**Primary complaint:** Panic confined to elevator use: tremor, vocalization, urination, and avoidance.

**Medical history as given in the lecture notes:** CBC, serum chemistry, and thyroid profile normal. No endocrinopathy and no neurologic deficit recorded.

**How a doctor frames it:** The stimulus is vestibular motion plus spatial confinement. That can activate the sympathetic branch of the autonomic nervous system (tachycardia, tremor, acute cortisol elevation as a stress response, not as a Cushing's diagnosis). Repeated pairing can strengthen an associative fear memory. The phenotype looks like "drama." The localization is context-specific autonomic reactivity, not generalized anxiety, until you prove otherwise.

**Pause and think**

1. Write a problem representation in one sentence. Include species, age, the exact context, and the four body signs.
2. What would make you abandon a purely situational diagnosis tonight? Name history and exam findings, not a drug.
3. Why is a normal thyroid panel necessary here, and why is it not sufficient?
4. Design a desensitization plan in four steps that does not start with a daily SSRI.
5. If the panic generalizes to hallways and cars, what changes in your plan, and what does not?

**Management principles (not a recipe):**

- Behavioral desensitization and counterconditioning to the elevator context come first.
- Vestibular habituation exercises are a sensory plan, not a personality plan.
- Short-term situational anxiolysis and, if generalization occurs, a serotonergic trial are hospital-protocol conversations. Do not copy a milligram-per-kilogram from a slide.
- Reassess after the context is trained. If the dog is still panicking in one place only, you have not failed medically. You have more training to do. If the dog is now panicking everywhere, reopen the medical screen.

**Teaching point:** Environment-specific autonomic reactivity can mimic generalized anxiety. A normal CBC, chemistry, and thyroid panel do not make the physiology irrelevant. They tell you the physiology is the fear circuit, not an endocrinopathy.

### Stage 2. The polyuria workup (Apr to Jun 2022)

The problem list now contains hyperadrenocorticism (entered 27 Apr 2022) and diabetes insipidus (entered 18 May and again 26 May 2022). | Differential for polyuria and polydipsia | What would support it | What you would run |

### Stage 3. The urinalysis said UTI, but the kidneys were fine (Jun 2022)

This is the stage the lecture wants you to be able to teach back.

**What the primary-care chart actually says, in order:**

- **2 Jun 2022:** cystocentesis. Urine culture with urinalysis submitted to Antech.
- **6 Jun 2022:** the clinician reports that the laboratory isolated a hemolytic *Streptococcus*. The owner is told Coco is a little better on an increased dose of "meds." Sensitivity is still pending. Enalapril 5 mg twice daily is dispensed the same day. The indication is not documented.
- **7 Jun 2022:** a second telephone note says staphylococcal UTI, sensitive to cefpodoxime. A 14-day course of cefpodoxime 100 mg once daily is started. The two organism names do not agree.
- **9 Jun 2022:** the owner reports that Coco is not finishing breakfast and walked away from dinner. The clinician suspects the antibiotic.
- **13 Jun 2022:** Coco is lethargic. The owner is told to stop **all medication**, including the trilostane (Vetoryl) that treated her Cushing's.
- **14 Jun 2022:** three blood-pressure readings: 87/50, 105/76, and 101/75 mmHg.
- **22 Jun 2022:** urine specific gravity **1.008**. Desmopressin ophthalmic drops started that day. Another culture-if submitted. Weight 29.6 lb.
- **24 Jun 2022:** repeat urinalysis reported with **no bacteria**. The owner says she is doing much better on the desmopressin drops and that PU/PD is less.

**Renal values across the whole record stay in the non-azotemic range until the last illness:** creatinine 0.3 mg/dL (Jan 2022), 0.4 to 0.6 mg/dL (Jun 2023 IDEXX comparisons), 0.8 mg/dL (15 Aug 2023). BUN 14 (Jan 2022), 21 and 21 (13 and 22 Jun 2023), 41 (15 Aug 2023, first high BUN). Phosphorus is not high in 2022.

**Pause and think**

1. How does a dog with a bacterial UTI have a normal creatinine and BUN? What does a normal creatinine fail to exclude?
2. Why is cystocentesis the preferred sample, and what would a free-catch sample change?
3. Dilute urine and glucocorticoid excess both predispose to UTI. Explain why. Does that change how long you treat?
4. Distinguish bacterial cystitis, pyelonephritis, subclinical bacteriuria, and sterile cystitis. Which would you treat, and on what evidence?
5. Lethargy, inappetence, and low-normal blood pressure appeared within weeks of several new drugs. List every plausible cause and say how you would separate them.
6. A UTI is treated and her renal values are normal. Is the polyuria explained? Why or why not?
7. The chart names two different organisms one day apart. What do you write in the problem list tonight: "UTI," "possible UTI," or "conflicting culture reports"?

**What a doctor does not do with this stage:** treat the UTI, see the creatinine stay "normal," and close the polyuria. Lower urinary infection can sit on top of dilute urine. It does not create eight cups of water a day by itself unless you have also proved pyelonephritis or another concentrating defect.
